# Maillard reaction

A dry surface browns and builds new flavor in the Maillard reaction, which
happens around 150ºC (300ºF). Water on the surface holds it near 100ºC (212ºF)
while it boils off, so a wet surface steams and turns gray before it can
brown.[^aoki]

## Levers

- **Surface moisture:** the surface can't brown until its water is gone.[^aoki]
- **Crowding:** food releases moisture as it cooks, and pieces packed together
    trap it and steam.[^mcneill]
- **Temperature:** a pan for searing goes to 204-232ºC (400-450ºF) before the
    meat goes in.[^earl]
- **Time:** the longer pecans roast at 175ºC (347ºF), the more pyrazines, the
    nutty products of the reaction, they develop.[^gong]

## Methods

### Searing

Searing builds a crust on a surface dry enough to brown, in a pan hot enough to
drive off any water quickly.[^aoki][^earl]

A sauce browns the same way. Soy sauce poured down the hot side of a wok browns
as its water boils off against the metal; poured into the food, it sits among
wet ingredients near 100ºC (212ºF), and the stir-fry loses its _wok hei_.[^lieu]

[Braised beef](../recipes/meats/beef/braised-beef.md),
[osso buco](../recipes/meats/pork/osso-buco.md), and
[sauerbraten](../recipes/meats/beef/sauerbraten.md) sear the meat before it goes
into liquid that will never get hot enough to brown it.

### Roasting

Roasting depends on dry heat reaching every piece so its surface can dry out and
brown.[^mcneill]

### Sautéing

Sautéing cooks food quickly in a small amount of fat over high heat, tossing or
flipping it in the pan as it cooks.[^gisslen] Oil shimmers at about 135ºC
(275ºF), hot enough for vegetables, and starts to smoke at about 204ºC (400ºF),
the cue for meat.[^atk]

### Pan-frying

Pan-frying cooks food in just enough oil to cover the bottom of the pan, at
about 170ºC (340ºF) for meat and 190ºC (375ºF) for vegetables.[^remley] The food
stays in place rather than being tossed, usually for longer and at lower heat
than a sauté.[^gisslen]

### Toasting

Toasting browns dry food, like nuts, seeds, spices, grains, and bread, in dry
heat or a little fat. Whole spices hold more of their oils than ground ones, so
they toast better,[^miller] and are ground after toasting.[^card] Burnt spices
taste harsh and bitter.[^miller]

### Sweating

Sweating keeps the reaction from starting. Vegetables cook slowly in a little
fat over low heat until they soften and turn translucent.[^nyorani] Salt draws
their water out by osmosis,[^rothman] and a lid keeps that water in the pan.
While the pan stays wet, the vegetables can't get much hotter than 100ºC
(212ºF). Color in the pan means the heat is too high.
