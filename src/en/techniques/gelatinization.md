# Gelatinization

Starch thickens a liquid as it heats in it, then thins it again if cooked too
long.[^leung]

## Levers

- **Heat:** a sauce thickened with starch has to come back to a simmer before it
    can be judged.[^leung]
- **Browning:** frying flour in fat breaks down its starch chains, so a darker
    roux thickens less.[^atk]
- **Starch:** tapioca starch thickens much like cornstarch, and potato starch
    works in a pinch. Flour clouds a sauce that cornstarch would leave
    glossy.[^leung]

## Methods

### Roux

Against a white roux cooked 1 minute, a blond roux cooked 3 minutes has 14% less
thickening power, and a peanut butter-colored roux cooked 5 minutes has 26%
less. The darker roux tastes stronger.[^atk]

### Slurry

Starch stirred into an equal amount of water and drizzled into a sauce thickens
it without powdery lumps. Starch settles out of the water quickly, so re-stir
the slurry right before it goes in.[^leung]

Hot bacon vinaigrette uses a slurry alongside
[mustard emulsification](emulsification.md#mustard-emulsification).

### Velveting

Meat for a stir-fry is marinated with water, seasoning, starch, and oil, then
par-cooked in oil or water before it meets the wok. The starch coating holds the
marinade in and shields the meat from the wok's heat, so it comes out
silky.[^leung-velveting] Tapioca starch makes an even silkier marinade than
cornstarch.[^leung]
