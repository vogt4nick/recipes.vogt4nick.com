# Starch

Starch thickens a liquid as it heats in it, then thins it again if cooked too
long.[^leung] Browning flour in fat before the liquid goes in breaks its starch
chains, so a darker roux thickens less.[^atk] In a sauce of grated cheese and
water, starch concentration is the main factor that keeps the cheese from
clumping as the sauce heats.[^bartolucci]

## Roux

Against a white roux cooked 1 minute, a blond roux cooked 3 minutes has 14% less
thickening power, and a peanut butter-colored roux cooked 5 minutes has 26%
less. Frying flour in fat breaks down its starch chains. The darker roux tastes
stronger.[^atk]

## Slurry

Starch stirred into an equal amount of water and drizzled into a sauce thickens
it without powdery lumps. Let the sauce come back to a simmer before judging it.
Starch settles out of the water quickly, so re-stir the slurry right before it
goes in. Tapioca starch thickens much like cornstarch. Potato starch works in a
pinch. Flour clouds a sauce that cornstarch would leave glossy.[^leung]

Hot bacon vinaigrette uses a slurry alongside
[mustard emulsification](emulsification.md#mustard-emulsification).

## Velveting

Meat for a stir-fry is marinated with water, seasoning, starch, and oil, then
par-cooked in oil or water before it meets the wok. The starch coating holds the
marinade in and shields the meat from the wok's heat, so it comes out
silky.[^leung-velveting] Tapioca starch makes an even silkier marinade than
cornstarch.[^leung]

The coating does the work that [gentle heat](heat/index.md) does elsewhere.
